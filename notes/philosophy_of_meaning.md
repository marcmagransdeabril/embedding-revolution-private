If we equate Frege’s sense (Sinn) to an embedding vector, that vector captures the semantic meaning, relationships, and "mode of presentation" of a concept or sentence within a mathematical space—just as a Fregean thought (Gedanke) represents the objective, shared content of a sentence.   However, an embedding vector only tells us what a sentence means (its internal semantic relationships), not whether it corresponds to reality. Frege argues that we need truth values (the True and the False) because meaning alone does not give us knowledge or anchor language to the real world. 

Frege states that "the mere thought alone yields no knowledge, but only the thought together with its reference, i.e. its truth value."   Sense (Embedding Vector): Tells you how concepts relate to each other inside a linguistic or conceptual system (e.g., knowing that "morning star" and "evening star" have different contextual associations, or that "unicorns" are associated with "horns" and "horses").   Truth Value (Reference): Acts as the bridge to objective reality. When we move from merely entertaining a thought to judging it to be true or false, we are testing whether that semantic representation actually holds in the world.   3. Aesthetic Appreciation vs. Scientific InquiryFrege notes that we do not always need truth values. When reading poetry or fiction, we are satisfied with the sense (the thoughts) and the subjective ideas (imagery and feelings) they evoke. *Plan (the author's notes above, to be rewritten as prose, about 1 page). Frege's three levels of difference give the kinds of likeness, each with its judge: the idea (free association), the sense (similarity, synonymy) and the reference (the world: morning star and evening star) [@frege1892sense]. A space trained on text reaches the sense at best, and the vector gives the thought, not its truth value. Three origins, one line each. Synonymy has no criterion independent of the judge, so likeness is graded [@quine1951two]. Reference is fixed by the world and not by the words around a name, so near is not alike [@kripke1980naming; @putnam1975meaning]. Likeness needs no feature shared by all members, only family resemblance [@wittgenstein1953philosophical].*

## Frege: three further points from the text

*Three further points from a reading of Frege's text itself (checked against the PDF in `tmp/toread`, translation of Geach and Black; page numbers of the printed translation still to be recorded). (1) The telescope: the Moon is the reference, the real image in the object glass is the sense, the retinal image of each observer is the idea. The image "is indeed one-sided and dependent upon the standpoint of observation; but it is still objective, inasmuch as it can be used by several observers". A learned vector is the object-glass image and not the retinal one: one-sided (it depends on the corpus, the loss and the seed, which is the subject of @sec-likeness-many-spaces), yet shared between users. Free association is the retinal image, and the reason an association judge is not a judge of sense. (2) The identity $a = a$ against $a = b$: the two differ in cognitive value, since the second "often contain\[s\] very valuable extensions of our knowledge". In a space the score of a name with itself is the maximum and the score of two names of one object is not, so $1 - \cos(\text{morning star}, \text{evening star})$ is a crude measure of how informative the identity is; a test that needs no model beyond the encoder, using pairs of co-referring names, and a check on the first axiom of @sec-likeness-not-distance. (3) The thought changes when a word is replaced by another of the same reference ("morning star" for "evening star"), "since one who did not know the identity might hold the one thought true and the other false", although the truth value does not change. So substitution of co-referents leaves truth and reference alone and moves the vector, which is the right behaviour for a space of sense and shows that the nearness of the two sentences is a nearness of sense and not of truth. The opposite case is quotation and "believes": there the words "do not have their customary reference but designate what is usually their sense" (indirect reference). A contextual vector sees the quotation marks and the verb; whether it moves the word towards its sense (the words around "Odysseus" in the quote) is untested and is noted for @sec-ch-contextual-vectors. Frege also allows sense without reference ("Odysseus", "the least rapidly convergent series"), which gives the probe in the table of the next section its form: same frame, an empty name and a real one.*

## Harris

*Four more points from a reading of Harris in the scanned PDF (OCR, pages of the printed article). (1) He denies that a word has a unit of meaning to be found: "There is not even a one-to-one relation between the vocabulary and any independent classification of meaning", no word has "a single or central meaning, or even … a continuous or coherent range of meanings", because of "homonymity, borrowing, forgotten metaphors" (p. 152). A static vector is one point per word, an average over such accidents, so the first limit of nearness is stated by the person who proposed the idea, and it is the reason for @sec-ch-contextual-vectors. (2) The correlation of structure with meaning "is much greater when we consider connected discourse" (p. 152), which makes the sentence and the document, and not the word, the level at which the claim is easiest to keep (@sec-likeness-phrases-sentences). (3) The substitution test is independent of truth: "It is not a question of whether the above sentence with lawyer substituted is true; it might be true in some situation" (p. 157), so what the test measures is selection and not reference, the same separation as Frege's sense and reference above. (4) He compares the structure of language with "human response (e.g. association, transference)" and finds that it does not conform to it (p. 151), so even for him distribution is not association; this is the author's warrant for keeping a judge of association and a judge of similarity apart, though the judges themselves are later (@sec-prop-nearness).*

## Osgood

*Osgood's semantic differential is the oldest space of this chapter with a judge built in, and the reading below is from the PDF (printed pages of Osgood, Suci and Tannenbaum 1957 [@osgood1957measurement]). A concept is rated on bipolar scales ("polar, opposite-in-meaning adjectives", each "a straight line function that passes through the origin"), and "difference in the meaning between two concepts is … a function of the multidimensional distance between the two points" (p. 26). Four things are useful here. (1) The judge is the space: the coordinates are named (evaluation, potency, activity) and the axes come from factor analysis of human ratings, whereas the axes of a trained space are free up to a rotation (@sec-prop-invariance). In the first analysis evaluation takes almost 70 per cent of the common variance and the three factors about half of the total (p. 38), but the author says that the sampling of scales was then deliberately diversified and that the space then needs many more dimensions, so the dimensionality of a space of meaning depends on which scales are sampled, as the ranking of models depends on which benchmark pairs are sampled. (2) Distance against correlation: for a profile of $k$ scores the correlation is blind to level and scale, so concepts that "covary perfectly" despite "gross absolute discrepancies" all correlate at 1.00, and Love would be as similar to Hate as to Affection (pp. 90–91). He adopts the generalized distance $D_{ij}=\sqrt{\sum_j d_{ij}^2}$ and shows that on standardised profiles $D_{ij}=\sqrt{2k(1-r_{ij})}$ (p. 91), with the loss of information that standardising brings. This is the cosine against the Euclidean distance of @sec-reading-nearness-correctly in a different guise: a score that ignores the norm cannot tell a concept from an intensified version of it, and Love and Hate (an antonym pair on one evaluative axis, so opposite in direction and the same in company) are a test case for @sec-near-not-alike. (3) Direction and norm are given meanings: the direction from the origin is the quality and the distance from the origin the intensity (p. 26). In a trained space the norm is said to carry frequency (@sec-ch-embedding-spaces), and the origin is arbitrary, so whether the norm of a word vector tracks the intensity of a rated word (a judged extremeness, or the sentiment lexicon) is a cheap test that Osgood's own ratings would allow. (4) A human noise floor and a check on the geometry: Rowan's triad-based "similarity" space had only slight negative latent roots against a Euclidean representation (the largest in absolute value not above .10 for one of them, p. 144), and the random half-splits of his subjects gave distance matrices that correlated .983 and .975 (p. 144), which is the human version of the seed null of @sec-likeness-many-spaces. The limits are Osgood's own: the rated concepts are few and chosen, the scales are assumed independent for $D$, and "the distribution of $D$ is not known" (in the section on differences between distances, page to be recorded), so he gives distances and not significance.*

## Medin, Goldstone and Gentner

*Medin, Goldstone and Gentner state the strongest objection to a single likeness score, and the reading below is from the PDF (OCR; pages of *Psychological Review* 100 [@medin1993respects]). Goodman called similarity "invidious, insidious, a pretender, an imposter, a quack" and argued that "A is similar to B" is "a blank to be filled" until one says in what respects, as motion needs a frame of reference [@goodman1972seven] (p. 254, where Mary says John is like Bill, and the respect is that both are chess players). A cosine has the same fault: it returns a number and not the respect, and the respect is whatever the loss and the corpus made salient. The authors answer that respects are not free but are fixed by the comparison itself, and propose that similarity is an alignment of structured parts, like analogy (Gentner's structure mapping [@gentner1983structure]), in which a match counts for more if it belongs to a larger consistent system, and the two things compared decide which of their features are activated (pp. 259–260). Three findings are usable as tests. (1) Context changes rank: judged alone, black and white (antonyms) get 2.2 on a scale from 1 (not similar at all) to 9 (highly similar), and 4.0 when black is compared with white and red in the same set; sunrise is rated more similar to sunbeam than to sunset when the pairs are separate (5.2 against 4.6) and the order reverses when the three words are shown together (sunset 6.4 against sunbeam 5.7); skin is nearer to bark than to hair when separate and the order reverses together (pp. 267–268). A static space gives one order for every context, so these are pairs for which no static space can match both conditions, and the antonym case is the one this chapter uses for @sec-near-not-alike: hot and cold are not simply "near but not alike", their likeness moves with what else is on the table. (2) The extension effect: Italy and Switzerland are judged less alike among European countries than among European and American ones, because a shared property that every member has carries no diagnostic value (p. 262, a statement of Tversky's diagnosticity). (3) Directionality and mutual constraint: comparing two concepts brings out features of one in the light of the other, as in metaphor ("both look like combs"), and the base is favoured over the target. The reply that follows for this chapter is a division of labour and not a defence of the cosine: a space gives the default respect, the one the training data made dominant, and a scorer that sees both items (a cross-encoder, MaxSim over token sets, @sec-likeness-other-objects) can in principle supply the respect that the comparison needs. How far it does is the question of @sec-likeness-phrases-sentences.*

## Before any model: what we mean by "alike" (the examples and Harris's ladder)

*Before any model, what we mean by "alike", with examples a reader can judge: dog and puppy (alike), dog and wolf (alike in a different way), dog and bark (related, not alike), dog and car (not alike), hot and cold (opposite, and in some respects alike). Similarity, relatedness and association are not the same relation: coffee and cup are related and not alike, car and train are alike. Human judgements are not always symmetric or metric [@tversky1977features] (the three axioms, and which trained scores keep them, are in @sec-likeness-not-distance), and the benchmark that names the relation (@sec-prop-nearness: WordSim-353 against SimLex-999 [@hill2015simlex; @faruqui2016problems]) decides what a space is credited with. Then what we would like to see in a space: alike things near, different things far, the order of the neighbours agreeing with a person's, and the same at every level, from a word to a clip of sound. The judge and its ceiling (the agreement between human raters) come before any model. Harris is the first to grade likeness by the overlap of environments, and his scale can serve as the section's ladder of "alike" [@harris1954distributional, §2.3, p. 157]: identical environments throughout (free variants); almost identical, except chiefly for sentences that contain both (synonyms: oculist, eye-doctor); some environments shared and some not (different meanings, oculist and lawyer, the difference corresponding roughly to the numerical relation of different to same environments); never the same environment (different grammatical classes). His judge is a procedure and not a rating: ask an informant to substitute any word for the one in the frame, without asking which words mean the same, and count what comes back. That is the operational form of the human judge of this section, and a nearest-neighbour list read against a substitution test is the cheapest version of it. To be written as one paragraph, with the sentence that Harris measures selection ("the probability of particular environments occurring with particular elements") and that his own estimate is rough, "almost no indication of its rarer selection" (p. 157), which is the reason a rare word has a poor vector and a first link to @sec-reading-nearness-correctly.*

## Wittgenstein

*Wittgenstein's *Philosophical Investigations* (1953 [@wittgenstein1953philosophical], read in the English text of the third edition, translated by Anscombe; cited by section) is quoted in this book for two claims, and both are more hedged and more specific than the quotations. (1) Use. §43 reads: "For a large class of cases—though not for all—in which we employ the word 'meaning' it can be defined thus: the meaning of a word is its use in the language", and the next sentence adds that "the meaning of a name is sometimes explained by pointing to its bearer". So the claim that likeness of use is likeness of meaning holds for a class of cases and is not a theory of all meaning. §§28–30 give the point that Goodman's "respects" repeat: "an ostensive definition can be variously interpreted in every case", and "how he 'takes' the definition is seen in the use that he makes of the word defined". (2) Family resemblance. §66 says "don't say: 'There must be something common' ... but look and see", and the result of looking at games is "a complicated network of similarities overlapping and criss-crossing: sometimes overall similarities, sometimes similarities of detail"; §67 names it ("I can think of no better expression ... than 'family resemblances'") and gives the image of the thread: "the strength of the thread does not reside in the fact that some one fibre runs through its whole length, but in the overlapping of many fibres", with the reply to the proposal of a common property as the disjunction of all the properties: "Now you are only playing with words". In a space this has a precise form, since a chain of near neighbours reaches points that are not near to the start, so that a family is a connected set under nearness and not a ball: it is the non-transitivity that Tversky's triangle example and Goodman's three discs show, and it is the reason a fixed radius around a prototype is a poor model of a concept. (3) Boundaries. §68–§71 say that the concept of a game "is not closed by a frontier", that "we can draw a boundary—for a special purpose", that a measure that was not exact was "an inexact one" ("you still owe me a definition of exactness"), and that a blurred concept is still a concept ("Is an indistinct photograph a picture of a person at all? ... Isn't the indistinct one often exactly what we need?", against Frege's comparison of a concept to an area with sharp boundaries, §71). A threshold $\epsilon$ on a cosine is such a boundary drawn for a purpose, and the rank $k$ of @sec-reading-nearness-correctly is another; neither is the concept. (4) Likeness is noticed and is not in the data: Part II §xi separates "What do you see there?" from "I see a likeness between these two faces", "the difference of category between the two 'objects' of sight": one man draws the faces accurately and "the other notice[s] in the drawing the likeness which the former did not see", which he calls noticing an aspect. A vector, or a pair of vectors, is what the first man draws; the likeness is a relation that a judge, or a trained score, notices, so the judge is not a detail of the evaluation of this chapter and is part of what likeness is.*

## Firth

*Firth's "A Synopsis of Linguistic Theory, 1930–1955" (1957 [@firth1957synopsis], read in the PDF of 18 pages, *Studies in Linguistic Analysis*, pp. 1–32 of the volume) is the source of the sentence that this book and the literature quote, and the reading shows that the sentence says less than the slogan. (1) The sentence is in a passage on use: "As Wittgenstein says, 'the meaning of words lies in their use'", and then, of the word *ass*, "the word ass is in familiar and habitual company, commonly collocated with *you silly-*, *he is a silly-*, *don't be such a-*. You shall know a word by the company it keeps! One of the meanings of ass is its habitual collocation with such other words" (p. 11). The claim is that *one of the meanings* is a collocation. (2) Meaning is stated "at a series of levels" (context of situation, collocation, syntax and colligation, phonology), an "analytic dispersion of the statement of meaning", with no level a prerequisite of another (pp. 8–10), and collocation "is not to be interpreted as context, by which the whole conceptual meaning is implied" (p. 11). A space of company is therefore one level of Firth's own account, and the "context of situation", with its participants, their social frameworks and the type of speech function, is the level that a text corpus does not carry (the Kaplan paragraph below makes the same point from logic). (3) He is not a statistician: he deplores "the piling-up of trivialities to be treated statistically, perhaps with defective theoretical principles" among "scientistic technicians" (p. 1), so the line from Firth to a matrix of counts is Harris's and Deerwester's and not his. (4) He foresees the clustering step: "meaning by collocation will suggest a small number of groups of collocations for each word studied. The next step is the choice of definitions for meanings suggested by the groups" (p. 12), that is, sense induction from contexts, which is what the many vectors of a contextual model per word do; and he insists on regarding "each word separately at first and not as a member of a paradigm" (p. 12), which sits uneasily with starting from a matrix of all words compared with each other (his "paradigm" is the grammatical one, so this is the author's reading and not his statement). (5) He distinguishes collocation from the lexicographer's citation, which is "keyed to the definitions" the lexicographer has "arbitrarily decided" to recognise, so a list of nearest neighbours read against a dictionary sense is a citation in his sense and not a collocation. The consequence for the chapter is a correction of attribution: "likeness of meaning shows as likeness of company" is Harris's measurable claim (his environments) and Firth's partial claim (one level of several), and the stronger claim that company is meaning is neither's.*

## Kaplan

*Kaplan's "Demonstratives" (1989 [@kaplan1989demonstratives], read in the PDF; page numbers of *Themes from Kaplan*) and the notes on Perry in the references (the file is a seven-page set of study notes by S. Marc Cohen, 2008, and not the 1979 paper, so Perry's examples are cited as reported there and the original is still to be supplied) change one claim of the Frege section. Kaplan separates two kinds of meaning that "Frege ... mixed together ... in what he called Sinn" (p. 501, note 26): *content*, "what is said", which is a proposition once the context is fixed and which is evaluated at circumstances, and *character*, "set by linguistic conventions" and "what is known by the competent language user", a function from contexts to contents (p. 505): "Meaning + Context → Intension; Intension + Possible World → Extension". For an indexical-free sentence ("fixed character", the eternal sentences) the two coincide; for "I", "here", "now" and "yesterday" they come apart, and the examples that follow are tests that a reader can run. (1) Same content, different characters: "Today is beautiful" said on Tuesday and "Yesterday was beautiful" said on Wednesday say the same thing, though one can lose track of the days and not realise it (pp. 501, 532–533), so two texts far apart in a space may express one content. (2) Same character, different contents: "I was insulted yesterday" said by two speakers or on two days says different things with identical words, so a text encoder will give them one vector, whatever the speaker (the test named in the table). (3) A sentence true in every context and yet contingent: "I am here now" "cannot be uttered falsely" and is logically true in his logic of demonstratives, while "David Kaplan is in Portland on 26 March 1977" is empirical (pp. 509–511): the truth value of that sentence is guaranteed by its character, and a space of company would have to put it near "I am not here now", which has almost the same company. The reading for this chapter is that a text space is a space of characters (cognitive significance), which is what the Frege reading in @sec-likeness-which already assumed, and that content, what is said by a use, is not in the vector. Perry's examples as reported in the notes (the shopper with a torn sack who realises "I am making a mess", the professor who believes the meeting starts at noon and moves only when he realises "the meeting starts now", the hiker who sets off when he thinks "this is the Mt. Tallac trail") show that the indexical belief cannot be replaced by a co-denoting non-indexical one without changing what is believed, and Kaplan quotes Perry that "it is the manner of presentation, the character and not the thought apprehended, that is tied to human action" (p. 532; the bear example: same character, same behaviour). For embeddings used to drive an agent (@sec-ch-multimodal-embeddings), the nearness that matters for action is of characters, and a robot's "I", "here" and "now" are inputs that no text corpus supplies.*

## Goodman

*Goodman's "Seven Strictures on Similarity" (1972 [@goodman1972seven], read in full, pages 437 to 446 of *Problems and Projects*) is the source of the "respects" row, and two of its arguments go further than the row says. (1) The count of shared features cannot define likeness (seventh stricture, pp. 443–444): in a universe of $n$ things, any two have exactly $2^{n-2}$ properties in common and $2^{n-2}-1$ that neither has, so two things are no more alike than any other two by the number of shared properties, and weighting them by importance "is a highly volatile matter, varying with every shift of context and interest". This is the answer to the question of how many dimensions of a trained space two items must share: the coordinates are not features, and a count or an unweighted sum of coordinates (a dot product) has no more claim than Goodman's count; the weights of a contrast model or of a learned projection are where the respect is (Tversky above; Peterson et al. in @sec-likeness-images-sounds-video). (2) Pairwise likeness does not give a common quality (sixth stricture, pp. 442–443): three discs, half red and half blue, half blue and half yellow, half yellow and half red, are each alike in pairs and have no colour common to all three, so "alike" is not transitive in the sense that classes need, and a cluster of neighbours of a neighbour of a point is not a class of things alike; this is Wittgenstein's family resemblance in an example that can be coded and run on a space (pairs near, triple with no shared direction). (3) Induction needs a choice of respect (fifth stricture, p. 441): the future "will be in some way like the past" whatever happens, so a prediction by nearest neighbours (retrieval, k-NN, in-context examples) is only as good as the respect in which the neighbours are near; for this book that is the link between @sec-ch-vector-databases and this chapter. (4) Metaphor and the reversal of explanation (fourth stricture, p. 440): "the fact that a term applies ... to certain objects may itself constitute rather than arise from a particular similarity among those objects"; this is the reading of a contrastive loss that this chapter uses for the built-in cell, where an image and its caption are near because the training pair says so. (5) Context moves the judgement and a laboratory scale governs it (p. 445): of three glasses, two of colourless liquid and one red, the first two are alike unless one of them holds hydrochloric acid and "I am thirsty"; and the psychophysical maps of similarity among sensory qualities "create rather than reflect a measure of sensory similarity", since there is no neutral ordinary condition against which to test them, a caution for any benchmark of similarity (@sec-likeness-judges), whose scores partly define what the models are trained to reproduce. (6) He does not conclude that similarity is useless: "serviceable in the streets", clear "when closely confined by context and circumstance", and "hopelessly ambiguous when torn loose" (pp. 444–446). This is the conditional claim of this chapter's working hypothesis, and "nearness is likeness locally and conditionally" is the same sentence with a space instead of a street.*

## Quine 1951, Putnam, Russell, Searle and Griffiths et al.

*Four of the origins in the table have now been read in the PDFs (the page references are those of the printed sources; Putnam's file holds only excerpts of the paper, 8 pages, so what is said of it is limited to them). Quine (1951 [@quine1951two]) argues that the notion of synonymy that analyticity needs "has still to be clarified", that a lexicographer's report of an observed synonymy "cannot be taken as the ground of the synonymy", and that our statements about the external world "face the tribunal of sense experience not individually but only as a corporate body" (about p. 38), which is the holism that the table assigns to a space; the text does not say that likeness is graded, and the step from "no criterion of synonymy" to "graded nearness" is the author's, to be kept as such. Putnam (1975 [@putnam1975meaning], excerpts) presents the extension and the intension of a term and the Twin Earth example "designed to show that meanings ain't in the head": a space trained on text has the head-side of this, the company of words, and the excerpts do not contain the division of linguistic labour, which is not claimed here. Russell (1905 [@russell1905denoting]) analyses phrases such as "the present King of France" as denoting phrases that may denote nothing, which is the case of a name with a sense and no reference that the Frege row already uses as a test. Searle (1980 [@searle1980minds]) is the Chinese room, a claim about programs and intentionality and not about the geometry of a representation; it is kept in the table only as the source of "form alone cannot give meaning" (with Bender and Koller [@bender2020climbing]), and not as evidence about nearness. Griffiths, Steyvers and Tenenbaum (2007 [@griffiths2007topics]) is the origin of the association tests of @sec-likeness-not-distance: they show that word association is asymmetric (keg and beer are given as examples), and violates the triangle inequality ("asteroid is highly associated with belt, and belt is highly associated with buckle, but asteroid and buckle have little association"; "such cases are the rule rather than the exception"), and that LSA, a cosine, cannot reproduce either, which a topic model, a probabilistic representation, can.*

## Quine 1960

*Quine's *Word and Object* (1960 [@quine1960word], read in the PDF of 309 pages, Chapter 2 "Translation and Meaning"; printed page numbers) is quoted by this chapter for the indeterminacy of translation, and the reading separates three things the table row had run together. (1) Stimulus meaning is the behavioural evidence, and it already falls short of synonymy: an informant's assent to "Gavagai?" "can depend excessively on prior collateral information" (p. 33), and "Gavagai" and "Rabbit" can have the same stimulus meaning as occasion sentences without the terms being coextensive: the objects may be "rabbits", "stages" or "undetached parts" of rabbits, the "fusion" of all rabbits, or rabbithood, and pointing does not separate them, since "point to a rabbit and you have pointed to a stage of a rabbit, to an integral part" and so on (pp. 46–47). The parallel in a space is that sameness of company, the "stimulus meaning" of a word in a corpus, fixes neither the extension nor the kind of term, and carries the collateral information of the community (the antonym case of @sec-likeness-not-distance is the same shortfall); this parallel is the author's reading and not a statement of Quine's. (2) The indeterminacy proper begins beyond that evidence: the finished manual maps every sentence, but "most of the semantic correlation is supported only by analytical hypotheses", and two systems of them "can fit the totality of speech behavior to perfection, and can fit the totality of dispositions to speech behavior as well, and still specify mutually incompatible translations of countless sentences insusceptible of independent control" (pp. 65–66, §§15–16). Quine adds that there is "not even ... an objective matter to be right or wrong about" (p. 66), unlike the translation of the occasion sentence. (3) He separates this from the superficial case: grammatical theories "can differ in word segmentations, in parts of speech ... and still have identical net outputs", but "I am talking of difference in net output" (p. 66). A rotation of a space is the first case, a change of description with the same net output, and it does not support Quine's thesis; his thesis would need two alignments of equal fit that differ in net output. Two further points keep the row honest. The thesis is stated against a total of dispositions to behaviour, and the evidence that two spaces trained on different languages align well is evidence about how much of the relational structure two corpora share, and not about the translation of sentences of radically different languages, so the row limits the evidence to the structure the two corpora share. And Quine notes that the manual's analogies are those of the linguist, "to impute our sense of linguistic analogy unverifiably to the native mind" (pp. 65–66); the choice of loss and of the anchor words in an alignment plays that role, and a unique answer from a procedure with those choices is not evidence of uniqueness of the data. The test that follows is the one added to the row: count distinct alignments with equal loss (different seeds, different anchor sets) and measure how often they disagree on the nearest neighbours of held-out words.*

## Kripke

*Kripke's *Naming and Necessity* (1980 [@kripke1980naming], lectures of 1970; read in the PDF of 180 pages, a scan read through OCR, with the printed page numbers; read are Lecture I and the start of Lecture II, pp. 48–60 and 78–97, and not the whole book) is the source of the row on reference, and it says more and less than the row. (1) What is attacked is a precise thesis: that the reference of a name is whatever uniquely, or mostly, satisfies the descriptions speakers associate with it. The counterexamples are cases of misinformation: if the only thing a speaker knows of Gödel is that he proved the incompleteness of arithmetic, and the work was in fact Schmidt's, the theory makes "Gödel" refer to Schmidt, "But it seems to me that we are not. We simply are not" (p. 84); likewise Peano, Einstein and Columbus, whose popular descriptions are false of them (p. 85). The theory also fails by circularity: a community cannot fix a reference by "the man to whom the incompleteness of arithmetic is commonly attributed" without "some independent criterion for the reference of the name" (p. 89). In a space the case is exact: the vector of "Gödel" is built from the company of the word, so if the company says Schmidt's deeds, the vector is near what the descriptions say, and it has no means to say that the name refers to someone else. (2) The positive picture is a chain: an initial "baptism" by ostension or by a description that fixes the reference, then passing the name "from link to link", the receiver intending "to use it with the same reference as the man from whom he heard it" (p. 96); Kripke says "I may not have presented a theory" but "a better picture than that given by description theorists" (p. 97), with the conditions not yet sufficient, and that the notion of reference is not eliminated but presupposed. A corpus contains the links that are written, and not the intention nor the baptism, so the chain is mostly outside the data; the reading of a space as a record of the chain is the author's. (3) Fixing a reference is not giving a meaning: a description can fix the reference of a name without being synonymous with it (the metre, Hesperus, pp. 56–58); names are "rigid designators", which designate the same object in every possible world where it exists (pp. 48–49, the President of 1970 is not rigid and Nixon is). This is the reason that a nearest neighbour such as "the author of the Critique" is not the same kind of thing as the name, and for the test of the row: swap the description and keep the person, and a space that tracks the description moves while a referent does not. (4) What the chapter should not claim: that a space fails on every name. Kripke grants that "in some cases" the reference is fixed by a description, and says that description theory is primarily applicable to the initial baptism (p. 96, note 42); for the large class of names whose corpus company is true of the bearer, the space and the chain agree, and the failure is where company is false or where two bearers share a name (his George Smith case, p. 97, is a case of two bearers and one name, which a single static vector averages).*

## The three groups: what fits, what is missing, what is open

The first group, **what fits**, is the distributional core: Frege's sense, Quine's holism and Wittgenstein's use all say that what a sign means is what is around it, and a space built from company is that claim made into a matrix. This is also the reason to be careful. A theory that a space fits by construction is not supported by the fit, since the loss was made to give it. The informative rows are the ones where the theory says that something should be missing, which is the second group.

The second group, **what is missing**, is reference. Kripke, Putnam and the Frege of the truth value agree that a sense alone is not knowledge of the world, and a text-only space has only the sense. This is the chapter's reason to separate what a space is credited with from what it is trusted for, and it is the philosophical form of "near is not alike" (@sec-near-not-alike). Two sources of the book already take sides: that form alone cannot give meaning [@bender2020climbing; @searle1980minds], and that symbols must be grounded in something outside the symbols [@harnad1990symbol].

The third group, **what is open**, has two questions. First, whether a text-only space can reach the reference, or only a better sense: the claim that separately trained models converge on a shared structure of the world is made [@huh2024platonic], and the reply is that what converges is mostly the local neighbourhood, not the global geometry [@groger2026aristotelian; @koepke2026plato]; whether grounding in images and action closes the gap is taken up in @sec-ch-multimodal-embeddings. Second, whether a "truth direction" tracks facts or only what the text asserts: there are linear structures of truth with causal interventions [@marks2024geometry] and unsupervised directions [@burns2023discovering], and also probes that fail to generalise [@levinstein2023still] and a warning that the method may find the most prominent feature and not truth [@farquhar2023challenges]. A direction that separates true from commonly asserted is a test of the first reading, and a direction that separates false from not said is a test of the third region (`notes/ideas.qmd`). In philosophical terms the first reading is correspondence to facts (a theory of truthmakers, Armstrong, to add), and the second is coherence with what the text says, or the deflationist view that "true" only endorses a sentence ([@ramsey1927facts, p. 157]: "it is true that Caesar was murdered" means no more than that Caesar was murdered). A claim that models' truth directions diverge from what is commonly believed has no source in the collection yet, and it is not to be stated before one is read; Frege's "indirect reference" (words in quotation or after "believes") is a different matter and is not the distinction between true and customary.
