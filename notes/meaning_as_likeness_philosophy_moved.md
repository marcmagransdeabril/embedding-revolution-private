# Meaning as Likeness: philosophical survey and judges (moved out of the chapter)

Verbatim copy of the opening survey (lines 9-51), "What Is Likeness in Meaning?" and "Kinds of Likeness and Where the Judges Come From" before they were consolidated into one short section. Not part of the book; the source details (page numbers, judges, ceilings) are kept here for the sections that need them.

Philosophy has given several answers to what meaning is. For example, Frege in "Sense and Reference" [@frege1892sense]:

> The difference may concern at most the ideas, or the sense but not the reference, or, finally, the reference as well.

separates the idea in our heads, the sense that is shared by all who use the sign, and the reference, the object in the world the sign represents (e.g., the Morning Star and the Evening Star are one planet, Venus, presented in two ways, and each of us pictures it differently); Harris in "Distributional Structure" [@harris1954distributional]:

> In other words, difference of meaning correlates with difference of distribution.

and Firth in "A Synopsis of Linguistic Theory, 1930–1955" [@firth1957synopsis]:

> You shall know a word by the company it keeps!

and also Wittgenstein in the *Philosophical Investigations* [@wittgenstein1953philosophical, §43]:

> for a large class of cases—though not for all—in which we employ the word "meaning" it can be defined thus: the meaning of a word is its use in the language.

seem to say that a sign means what it does through its use and its company, their co-occurrence (e.g., oculist and eye-doctor turn up in nearly the same sentences, oculist and lawyer in very different ones); Quine in "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" [@quine1951two]:

> [O]ur statements about the external world face the tribunal of sense experience not individually but only as a corporate body.

says that the sense of a sentence is not something it carries by itself, because what we check against sense experience (what we see, hear and measure) is the whole system of our beliefs and never one sentence alone (an astronomer who finds a planet missing where a theory puts it can revise the theory, the telescope or the arithmetic, and the observation does not say which); Goodman in "Seven Strictures on Similarity" [@goodman1972seven]:

> Similarity, ever ready to solve philosophical problems and overcome obstacles, is a pretender, an impostor, a quack.

and Wittgenstein again [@wittgenstein1953philosophical, §66], who sees in the games

> a complicated network of similarities overlapping and criss-crossing

object that likeness is not a count of shared properties, since things can be alike with nothing in common to all of them (e.g., chess, patience and football are all games, and no one feature runs through all three) and any two things share countless properties until we say in what way they are being compared (by colour, by shape, by use); Kripke in *Naming and Necessity* [@kripke1980naming, p. 84], against the view that a name means the descriptions we attach to it. Suppose all that an ordinary speaker knows of Gödel is that he proved the incompleteness of arithmetic, and that the proof was in fact Schmidt's. Then, on that view:

> [S]ince the man who discovered the incompleteness of arithmetic is in fact Schmidt, we, when we talk about 'Gödel', are in fact always referring to Schmidt. But it seems to me that we are not. We simply are not.

Kripke goes on to say that a name is tied to the thing it names, through the chain of speakers who passed it on from the first use [@kripke1980naming, p. 96], and not to the descriptions we attach to it; Putnam in "The Meaning of 'Meaning'" [@putnam1975meaning], who imagines a Twin Earth where what looks and tastes like water is not $H_2O$ but another substance, and a man on Earth and his exact duplicate there:

> Oscar~1~ and Oscar~2~ understood the term "water" differently in 1750 although they were in the same psychological state

says that what a word means is not fixed by what is in the speaker's head; Kaplan in "Demonstratives" [@kaplan1989demonstratives]:

> The character of an expression is set by linguistic conventions and, in turn, determines the content of the expression in every context.

says that what a sentence says depends on who says it and where ("I am here now" says something different each time).

All these examples cover different interpretations of meaning, most likely covering different aspects of how likeness can be understood. In this and the following chapters, we will examine how these interpretations manifest in embedding spaces and what they reveal about the nearness of meaning. How many of these interpretations are captured and which are missed will be a central question throughout the text.

## What Is Likeness in Meaning? {#sec-likeness-which}

The introduction ended with a list of questions, and each of them presupposes an answer to an earlier one: what would it mean for two things to be alike in meaning? Before any space is measured we should ask what each philosophical account says a likeness of meaning would have to be, so that later sections can test whether an embedding space captures it.

We read each account for the same three things: what it says a likeness of meaning would be, what a vector with a distance can carry of it, and where the vector stops. The accounts are not tested against one another. A theory that a space fits by construction is not supported by the fit, because the loss was made to give it, so the informative cases are the ones where the theory says that something should be missing, and each of them ends in a hand-off to the property that takes over.

Frege is the natural start, because he separates three levels at which expressions differ: "The difference may concern at most the ideas, or the sense but not the reference, or, finally, the reference as well" [@frege1892sense]. Each level has its judge. The idea is the private image, and the judge that comes closest is free association; the sense is shared and is judged by similarity and synonymy; the reference is the object, and the judge is the world. His telescope puts the three apart: the Moon is the reference, the real image in the object glass is the sense, and the image on each observer's retina is the idea. The image in the glass "is indeed one-sided and dependent upon the standpoint of observation; but it is still objective, inasmuch as it can be used by several observers". A learned vector is the image in the glass. It is one-sided, since it depends on the corpus, the loss and the seed, and it is shared among its users; free association is the retinal image, which is why a judge of association is not a judge of sense. A text-only space reaches the sense at best: the vector gives the thought and does not give its truth value, and Frege adds that "the mere thought alone yields no knowledge, but only the thought together with its reference, i.e. its truth value". How far a space can go from the sense to the reference is the subject of the chapter on reference and truth.

If sense is what a vector can hold, the next question is where sense comes from, and Harris and Firth answer with company. Harris holds that "difference of meaning correlates with difference of distribution": oculist and eye-doctor occur in almost the same environments, oculist and lawyer in many different ones, and the amount of difference in meaning corresponds roughly to the amount of difference in environments [@harris1954distributional, pp. 156–157]. He claims less than the slogan. No word has "a single or central meaning, or even … a continuous or coherent range of meanings" (p. 152), the correlation is "much greater when we consider connected discourse" (p. 152), and the substitution test measures selection and not truth: "it is not a question of whether the above sentence with lawyer substituted is true or not" (p. 157). Firth's sentence, "You shall know a word by the company it keeps!", sits in a passage on use, and the next one says that "one of the meanings of ass is its habitual collocation with such other words" (p. 11), so a collocation is one meaning among several [@firth1957synopsis]. He states meaning "at a series of levels", of which collocation is one and the context of situation another (pp. 8–10), and he deplores "the piling-up of trivialities to be treated statistically" (p. 1). The measurable claim, that likeness of meaning shows as likeness of company, is Harris's; the partial claim, that company is one level of meaning, is Firth's; the claim that company is meaning is neither's. A space built from counts or predictions of company is this measurable claim made into a matrix, so that the likeness it shows is the loss and not a discovery. What it cannot carry is Firth's other levels, above all the context of situation (who speaks, to whom, about what), which a corpus of text does not hold, and the dependence of a word's meaning on its sentence, which is the property of context.

Use is also where Wittgenstein starts, but he draws a conclusion that matters for the shape of a space. For "a large class of cases—though not for all—in which we employ the word 'meaning' it can be defined thus: the meaning of a word is its use in the language" (§43), so likeness of use is likeness of meaning for a class of cases and not a theory of all meaning [@wittgenstein1953philosophical]. The members of a class like the games need share no feature: they form "a complicated network of similarities overlapping and criss-crossing" (§66), a "family resemblance" (§67), like a thread whose strength "does not reside in the fact that some one fibre runs through its whole length, but in the overlapping of many fibres". The concept "is not closed by a frontier" (§68): one can draw a boundary "for a special purpose", and a blurred concept is still a concept. Goodman states the same from the side of logic [@goodman1972seven]. In a universe of $n$ things any two have exactly $2^{n-2}$ properties in common, so counting shared properties cannot define likeness, and weighting them "is a highly volatile matter, varying with every shift of context and interest" (pp. 443–444). Three discs, half red and half blue, half blue and half yellow, half yellow and half red, are alike in pairs and have no colour common to all three (pp. 442–443). In a space this means that nearness is not transitive, that a family is a connected set under nearness and not a ball around a prototype, and that a fixed radius is a poor model of a concept; a threshold is a boundary drawn for a purpose, and neither it nor a rank is the concept. A cosine also returns a number and not the respect in which the two items are alike, which is the one that the loss and the corpus made dominant. Wittgenstein adds that likeness is noticed and is not in the data: the man who draws two faces accurately and the one who notices their likeness see different things (Part II, §xi), so the judge is part of what likeness is.

The relations that Wittgenstein leaves open are the ones that Quine ties together. He argues that the notion of synonymy that analyticity needs "has still to be clarified", and that our statements about the external world "face the tribunal of sense experience not individually but only as a corporate body" (about p. 38) [@quine1951two]. This is the holism that a space fits: the position of a word is defined by its relations to all the others. The text does not say that likeness is graded, and that step is the author's. In *Word and Object* the evidence for translation is stimulus meaning, and it already falls short of synonymy: assent to "Gavagai?" "can depend excessively on prior collateral information" (p. 33), and "Gavagai" and "Rabbit" can have the same stimulus meaning without being coextensive terms, since the objects may be rabbits, rabbit stages, undetached parts, or rabbithood (pp. 46–47) [@quine1960word]. The indeterminacy begins beyond that evidence: rival manuals of translation "can fit the totality of speech behavior to perfection, and can fit the totality of dispositions to speech behavior as well, and still specify mutually incompatible translations of countless sentences insusceptible of independent control" (pp. 65–66). He sets apart the weak case, in which systems differ "in word segmentations, in parts of speech" and still "have identical net outputs", from his own, which is "a difference in net output" (p. 66). The coordinates of a space are free up to a rotation, and a rotation is the weak case: it changes the description and leaves every relation unchanged. His thesis would need two alignments that fit the relations inside both spaces equally well and disagree on the neighbours of held-out words. The measure that fits holism is therefore one that a rotation does not change, the overlap of nearest neighbours between spaces and under removal of a part of the corpus, and the comparison of two spaces by alignment is a hand-off to the chapter on arrangement.

All these accounts take for granted that likeness is a relation that behaves like nearness, and experimental psychology is the strongest objection to that. Tversky showed that judged likeness violates the axioms of a distance, since it is not symmetric, not minimal and not transitive in the sense of the triangle inequality, because it weighs the common and the distinctive features of the two things with weights that depend on which is the subject and on the set compared [@tversky1977features]. A symmetric metric cannot say that $a$ is more like $b$ than $b$ is like $a$, and a two-tower score or a learned bilinear form can. Which axioms a trained space keeps is set by its score as much as by its data.

Sense and company still leave open what a name is about, and here Kripke and Putnam break with the descriptive tradition. Kripke attacks a precise thesis, that the reference of a name is whatever uniquely or mostly satisfies the descriptions that speakers associate with it [@kripke1980naming]. If the only thing a speaker knows of Gödel is that he proved the incompleteness of arithmetic, and the work was in fact Schmidt's, the theory makes "Gödel" refer to Schmidt, "But it seems to me that we are not. We simply are not" (p. 84); Peano, Einstein and Columbus are further cases (p. 85). The theory also fails by circularity, since a community cannot fix a reference by "the man to whom the incompleteness of arithmetic is commonly attributed" without "some independent criterion for the reference of the name" (p. 89). His positive picture is a chain from an initial baptism, each speaker intending "to use it with the same reference as the man from whom he heard it" (p. 96), which he offers as "a better picture" and not as a theory (p. 97). Names are rigid designators, which designate the same object in every possible world where it exists (pp. 48–49). Putnam's Twin Earth example is "designed to show that meanings ain't in the head" [@putnam1975meaning]. A space knows a name only through the words around it, which is close to the cluster-of-descriptions theory that Kripke rejects: the vector of "Gödel" is near the company of the name, and if the company says Schmidt's deeds the vector has no means to say that the name refers to someone else. The chain is mostly outside the data, since a corpus holds the links that were written and not the intention or the baptism. Kripke himself grants that the description theory applies to the initial baptism (p. 96, note 42), so for the many names whose company is true of the bearer the space and the chain agree, and the failures are where the company is false or where two bearers share a name. The result is handed to the chapter on reference and truth.

Reference depends on who is speaking, and Kaplan makes that dependence exact. A word has a rule that does not depend on the context (its character, a function from contexts to contents) and a content fixed in the context (pp. 504–505) [@kaplan1989demonstratives]. A text encoder takes the text and not the context of use, so it lives on the side of the character: "I", "here" and "now" get one vector whoever says them and wherever. A demonstration with no corpus is enough to show it, and the dependence of meaning on the situation of use is the subject of the chapter on context.

Ramsey brings the discussion back to truth and use. For him "p is true" is simply the same as p (p. 157), and the meaning of a sentence is "to be defined by reference to the actions to which asserting it would lead, or, more vaguely still, by its possible causes and effects" (p. 170) [@ramsey1927facts]. A space of company has no truth predicate to add, so a direction that separates true from false text is a direction of what is asserted, and on Ramsey's redundancy view that is all there is to a truth value. Whether such a direction tracks the facts or only what the text asserts is the question of the chapter on reference and truth.

Three further positions bear on where meaning could be anchored. Gärdenfors places meanings as regions of a space of quality dimensions and not as points [@gardenfors2000conceptual], which the chapter on regions takes up. Harnad holds that symbols must be grounded in something outside the symbols [@harnad1990symbol], and Bender and Koller that a system trained only on form has no way to learn meaning [@bender2020climbing]. Both bear on the separation of the nearness that a paired loss builds in, where an image and its caption are near because the training pair says so, from the nearness that appears unasked; only the second is evidence about meaning, and what a second sense adds is the subject of the chapter on senses.

Taken together, the accounts fit in one place and miss in another. What fits is the distributional core: Frege's sense, Quine's holism, Wittgenstein's use, Harris's and Firth's company all say that what a sign means is what is around it, and a space built from company is that claim made into a matrix. What is missing is reference. Kripke, Putnam and the Frege of the truth value agree that a sense alone is not knowledge of the world, and a text-only space has only the sense, which is the philosophical form of "near is not alike" (@sec-near-not-alike) and the reason to separate what a space is credited with from what it is trusted for. What is open is whether a text-only space can reach the reference or only a better sense: the claim that separately trained models converge on a shared structure of the world is made [@huh2024platonic], and the reply is that what converges is mostly the local neighbourhood and not the global geometry [@groger2026aristotelian; @koepke2026plato]. Whether grounding in images and action closes the gap is taken up in the chapter on senses; whether a truth direction tracks facts or only what the text asserts, in the chapter on reference and truth.

These claims are only useful if they can be checked, and a check needs a judge: someone or something that says whether two things are alike, and in which respect. The next section (@sec-likeness-judges) separates similarity, relatedness and association, which the accounts above use without distinction, and asks where the judges of each come from.

::: {.questions}
1. In Frege's telescope, what do the Moon, the real image in the object glass and the image on each observer's retina stand for, and which of them is a learned vector closest to? Why?

2. "You shall know a word by the company it keeps" is often quoted as if company were meaning. What does Firth say in the same passage, and what does Harris claim instead?
:::

::: {.answers}
1. The Moon is the reference (the object), the real image in the object glass is the sense, and the image on each observer's retina is the idea. Frege says the image in the glass "is indeed one-sided and dependent upon the standpoint of observation; but it is still objective, inasmuch as it can be used by several observers". A learned vector is closest to the image in the glass. It is one-sided, since it depends on the corpus, the loss and the seed, and it is shared among all the users of the space. Free association is the retinal image, which is why a judge of association is not a judge of sense.

2. Firth's sentence sits in a passage on use, and the next one says that one of the meanings of a word is its habitual collocation with other words, so collocation is one meaning among several. He states meaning at a series of levels (context of situation, collocation, syntax, phonology), and he is not a statistician. Harris makes the measurable claim, that difference of meaning correlates with difference of distribution (oculist and eye-doctor share almost all their environments, oculist and lawyer few), and he adds that he does not claim a full definition of meaning from it. Neither claims that company is meaning.
:::

## Kinds of Likeness and Where the Judges Come From {#sec-likeness-judges}

Here we check what we are asking of a space when we ask whether two things are alike. Similarity, relatedness and association are different questions with different judges, and a space can only be credited against the judge that matches the question.

Before any model, what we mean by "alike", with examples a reader can judge: dog and puppy are alike, dog and wolf are alike in a different way, dog and bark are related and not alike, dog and car are not alike, and hot and cold are opposite and in some respects alike. Three relations are mixed in these examples. Similarity is a correspondence between the properties of two words, relatedness is the wider notion of which similarity is a special case (cars and gasoline are more related than cars and bicycles, but the latter pair are more similar), and association is a tendency to occur together (bee and honey) [@budanitsky2006evaluating; @turney2010frequency]. The last two are independent: doctor and nurse are both similar and associated, horse and platypus are similar and not associated, cradle and baby are associated and not similar, and calculus and candy are neither. Relational similarity, between two pairs and not between two words, does not reduce to the first: mason and carpenter are alike, and so are stone and wood, yet mason : glass and carpenter : clay share no relation [@turney2010frequency]. Antonyms are "dissimilar and hence distant in one sense, and yet strongly related semantically and hence close in the other" [@budanitsky2006evaluating], so "near" has two readings even before there is a space, and a benchmark must say what it does with them.

A judge is a procedure that fixes which of these relations is being asked about, and what a space is credited with depends on it. Harris's judge is an informant who substitutes words in a frame, and his ladder of likeness is by overlap of environments: identical throughout (free variants), almost identical except for sentences that contain both (synonyms), some environments shared and some not (different meanings), and never the same (different grammatical classes) [@harris1954distributional, p. 157]. The standard rated judges differ in what the raters were asked. WordSim-353 asked for a "similarity" score on a scale anchored at 0 (totally unrelated) and 10 (very closely related) and told the raters to count antonyms as similar, so its confound between relatedness and similarity is in its design [@finkelstein2002placing], and SimLex-999 asked for similarity proper and carries the strength of free association (USF) of each of its pairs as a separate column [@hill2015simlex; @faruqui2016problems]. Budanitsky and Hirst give three ways to evaluate a measure: examine its mathematical properties, which acts "at best as a coarse filter"; compare it with human judgements, the best assessment "by definition" and limited to a few hundred pairs; and measure it in an application [@budanitsky2006evaluating, §3]. This chapter uses all three in that order: the axioms of @sec-likeness-not-distance are the coarse filter, the rated judges are the second, and the retrieval and classification tasks of the later chapters are the third.

The ceiling of a rated judge is the agreement between its human raters, and it is defined here as the mean correlation of one rater with the mean of the others, not as the score of an average test taker. Resnik replicated the Miller–Charles experiment with ten subjects and obtained 0.8848 [@resnik1995using], an estimate with its own spread and not a constant, and the ways of building the ceiling differ (a rater against the mean, pairwise agreement, split-half), so a result is reported with the one used. A method can exceed an average test taker without reaching the raters: Rapp's method scored 92.5 per cent on the TOEFL synonym items against 64.5 per cent for the average applicant [@turney2010frequency]. The statistics of the judges of each experiment are stated in the section that runs it.

::: {.questions}
1. Give a pair of words for each of the four cells of similar and not similar, associated and not associated.

2. Why is the ceiling of a judge defined as the agreement between raters and not as the score of an average test taker?
:::

::: {.answers}
1. Doctor and nurse are both similar and associated; horse and platypus are similar (both animals) and not associated; cradle and baby are associated and not similar; calculus and candy are neither [@turney2010frequency]. The same four cells can be built from the two columns of SimLex-999, the similarity rating and the strength of free association of each pair.

2. A mean score is a level to compare with and not an upper bound. Rapp's method scored 92.5 per cent on the TOEFL synonym items against 64.5 per cent for the average applicant, so a method can exceed the average test taker without reaching the raters [@turney2010frequency]. What a judge can give is the agreement among the people who built it, for example the mean correlation of one rater with the mean of the others. That number has its own spread, and several ways of computing it exist (a rater against the mean, pairwise, split-half), so a result states which one it uses.
:::
